Format and handicap rules

Event rules: two-man one net best, 85% allowance, Calcutta scored on Sunday only, top 5 owners plus ties paid, a tie for first settled on 18.

Saturday carry-over. After Round 1, enter each team's Saturday net score on its card. The model shifts that team's expected Sunday score by the weight above times the gap between what it shot and what the model expected. 20% is a reasonable weight for one round of evidence given that some handicaps are soft; 0% ignores Saturday.

Course handicaps use Greystone's ratings: Blue 72.1/140, Grey 71.2/135, White 70.4/132, Green 69.2/130, par 72, at the Round 1 tees.

How the numbers work

Odds. The model plays Sunday thousands of times. Each player gets a course handicap from his index and tee (Greystone ratings), then 85% of it as playing strokes, placed on holes by stroke index. On every hole each player draws a score from a distribution keyed to his handicap (how often a 15 makes birdie, par, bogey, double, worse), with a per-round form swing so hot and cold days happen. The team's hole score is the better net ball. Teams are ranked, the top 5 are paid with ties split, and a team's share is its average cut of the pot across all those Sundays.

Fair value = share × pot estimate (less any house take). It is what 100% of the team is worth to a risk-neutral buyer. Cap = fair, halved if the buyback puts the team's money into the pot, then less your target edge. EV of a position = your ownership × fair, minus your net cost. Market share is the team's money in the pot divided by the pot, so a team whose model share beats its market share was bought cheap.

Why teams differ. In one net best ball, what wins is a reliable net-par floor from one player plus net birdies from the other. The 85% allowance takes more strokes off high handicaps, so the best structure here is a very low index (loses almost nothing) with a 13 to 17 partner (keeps most of his strokes and supplies the birdies). Two similar mid handicaps with no spread, or a plus handicapper who gives strokes back, sit at the bottom. The gaps are small: the best team wins about 4.5% of the time, the worst about 1.5%.
